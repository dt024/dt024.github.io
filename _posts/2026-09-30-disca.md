A self-driving car's brakes fail. It can swerve and hit two elderly pedestrians, or stay its course and hit two young ones. There is no factual answer to this. There is only a **moral preference**, and people don't share one.

The Moral Machine experiment put dilemmas like this to people around the world and collected 40 million judgments across 233 countries. The answers differ by country: whether to favour the young over the old, the many over the few, the fit over the frail. Large language models, meanwhile, don't sit at a neutral point. Their default preferences land closest to Western, educated, industrialised, rich and democratic respondents. When one model is deployed everywhere, one population's moral defaults travel with it.

A model aligned to one population is, by the same measure, misaligned for the others. And the countries furthest from the model's default are usually the ones with the least data, compute and vendor access to fix it. In our NeurIPS 2026 paper {% cite huynh2026cultural %} we ask how to close that gap **using only what everyone has.**

## The constraint: a frozen model and public survey data

Today's fixes each need something most users don't have. Fine-tuning needs per-country preference data and a training run. Reward-guided decoding needs a reward model for every culture. Activation steering needs access to the model's internals. The cheapest fix, a prompt, isn't enough: describing a country's values to the model barely moves it, and asking it to answer "as a typical citizen" helps, but rests on a single point of view.

So we work under a deliberately strict constraint. The model's weights stay frozen. There is no reward model and no access to activations. The method never sees the human preferences we evaluate against. The only country-specific input is the public **World Values Survey**, and the only thing we read from the model is how strongly it favours one answer over the other.

## Three observations

The design comes from three observations.

1. **A persona is a strong push, and too strong.** Telling a model to answer as a person of a given age, income or religion shifts its moral decisions two to four times more than the same description shifts real people. A single persona overshoots, and it reflects whoever wrote the prompt more than the country as a whole.
2. **A country isn't a single point.** Survey data show that values within a country vary with generation and social position. A prompt describing one "typical citizen" throws that variation away.
3. **Pollsters solved this long ago.** A pollster doesn't trust one respondent. They ask many, report the average, and attach a margin of error. A difference smaller than the margin is treated as noise.

Put together, these change the question. Instead of asking _how do we push the model toward a country?_, we ask **_how far should any push be trusted?_** When the people we consult disagree, we should trust the fine print less.

## Ask four local guides

Picture asking four local guides the way to the station. You walk in the direction their answers average out to. If the four agree closely, you also let them talk you through the last few turns. If they disagree, you stop at the average and don't let any one of them talk you further.

DISCA (Disagreement-Informed Steering for Cultural Alignment) does exactly this with a frozen model.

For each country we build four personas from World Values Survey microdata: three age cohorts (young, middle-aged, older) and one country-wide persona. Each is a short prompt, written in the country's own language, that describes values such as religiosity, trust in strangers and views on gender roles at the levels the survey measured for that group. The differences can be surprisingly narrow. The four US personas differ in just two clauses, about national pride and moral acceptability, and that small difference is still enough to matter.

We then show the frozen model the same dilemma under each persona and read off how strongly it favours each option. The **consensus** of the four sets the direction and does most of the work. The **disagreement** between them decides how much of any further fine adjustment to keep. If the panel agrees, the adjustment is kept. If it is split, the adjustment fades away and the answer stays at the consensus.

Two things to notice. First, the big move is from the model's default to the panel's consensus; the four personas together pull the model far more reliably than any one of them could. Second, press "Panel splits": the consensus barely changes, but the meter collapses. When the people you consult disagree, DISCA stops trusting anything beyond their common ground.

## Leave no one behind

Averaging has a blind spot. Three cohorts mildly in favour and one strongly against average out to "mildly in favour", and the model moves in a direction one group rejects. So the fine adjustment around the consensus is **loss-averse**. It borrows the value curve from Kahneman and Tversky's Prospect Theory: a step that makes things worse for any one cohort counts more than an equally sized step that makes things better for another.

The adjustment is small by design. The consensus carries most of the correction, and the loss-averse step only fine-tunes around it, and only as much as the panel's agreement allows.

The map is mostly blue, across every region and every model. It isn't blue everywhere, and we keep the orange cells visible on purpose: some country–model pairs get worse, and the smallest model gains the least. One comparison stands out: a 14B model with DISCA ended up closer to people's measured preferences than a 70B model, five times its size, without it. The same signal also carries beyond crash dilemmas, to open-ended moral scenarios, questions about social norms, and free-form writing, although how much it helps depends on the task.

## Using it responsibly

Matching a country's survey average is not the same as being right. A majority view isn't automatically legitimate, safe or appropriate for every decision, and the personas describe populations, not any individual person. A system built on DISCA should respect an individual user's own preferences when it knows them, send high-stakes decisions to human experts, and treat strong disagreement as a reason to abstain or escalate, not as licence to impose the majority view.

## Limits

DISCA needs to read how strongly the model favours each answer option, so it works with models that expose these scores, not with ones that only return text. It needs survey coverage for the country in question. And it doesn't win everywhere: the orange cells above are real, and closing them is the next step.
