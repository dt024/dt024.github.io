A self-driving car's brakes fail. It can swerve and hit two elderly pedestrians, or stay its course and hit two young ones. There is no factual answer to this. There is only a **moral preference**, and people don't share one.

The Moral Machine, an online experiment run by MIT researchers, put dilemmas like this to people around the world and collected 40 million judgments across 233 countries. The answers differ by country: whether to favour the young over the old, the many over the few, the fit over the frail. Large language models (LLMs), meanwhile, don't sit at a neutral point. Their default answers to such dilemmas land closest to those of Western, educated, industrialised, rich and democratic respondents.

Why does that matter? Because these models no longer just answer trivia. They already sit inside decisions that weigh one person's interests against another's. A content moderator asks a model whether a post mocking a religion should stay up. A hospital assistant helps a family decide how much risk an ageing parent should be allowed to take. A car designer asks how a vehicle should weigh the people it could harm. None of these has a factual answer; each rests on a moral preference. When an AI is in the loop, the preference it was trained on becomes the default, and the person on the receiving end never sees it and can't change it.

"Aligning" a model means making it act on human values. But that goal only makes sense once we say _whose_ values. A model aligned with one population is, by the same measure, misaligned with everyone else, and because a single model serves people all over the world, its defaults quietly apply one population's values to billions of people who don't share them.

The cost also falls unevenly. The countries furthest from the model's defaults usually have the least data, computing power and access to AI companies needed to fix it. The gap is widest exactly where the means to close it are thinnest, so any fix has to work for them too. In our NeurIPS 2026 paper {% cite huynh2026cultural %} we ask how to close that gap **using only what everyone has.**

## The constraint: an unchanged model and public survey data

Existing fixes each need something most users don't have. Retraining the model on a country's preferences needs preference data from that country and an expensive training run. Guiding the model's output with a separate scoring model needs such a scoring model for every culture. Adjusting the model's internal computations needs access inside the model, which commercial AI services don't offer. The cheapest fix, simply instructing the model in the prompt, isn't enough: describing a country's values to the model barely changes its answers, and asking it to answer "as a typical citizen" helps but still rests on a single point of view.

So we work under a deliberately strict constraint. The model itself is never changed or retrained, there is no extra scoring model, and we never look inside it. The method also never sees the human answers we later use to evaluate it. The only country-specific input is the **World Values Survey**, a public survey that has asked representative samples of people in nearly 100 countries about their beliefs and values since 1981, from religion and family to trust and politics. And the only thing we read from the model is how strongly it prefers one answer option over the other.

## Three observations

The design comes from three observations.

1. **A persona is a strong push, and too strong.** A persona is an instruction such as "answer as a 30-year-old from Brazil". Telling a model to answer as a person of a given age, income or religion shifts its moral decisions two to four times more than the same description shifts real people's answers. A single persona overshoots, and it reflects whoever wrote the prompt more than the country as a whole.
2. **A country isn't a single point.** Survey data show that values within a country vary with generation and social position. A prompt describing one "typical citizen" throws that variation away.
3. **Pollsters solved this long ago.** A pollster doesn't trust one respondent. They ask many, report the average, and attach a margin of error. A difference smaller than the margin is treated as noise.

Put together, these change the question. Instead of asking _how do we push the model toward a country?_, we ask **_how far should any push be trusted?_** When the people we consult disagree, we should be careful about moving the model any further than their common ground.

## Ask four locals for directions

Imagine you're looking for the train station on a long, straight street, and you ask four locals how far to walk. One says five blocks, one says six, one says five, and one says three. You can't follow all four, so you start with the average: about five blocks.

- **If the four mostly agree**, as here, it's worth letting them bargain over the last bit, for example whether the station is just before or just after the fifth block, and adjusting where you stop to the spot they settle on.
- **If they disagree wildly**, say two blocks versus nine, letting them bargain would only restart the argument. So you walk the average distance and stop there.

Our method, DISCA (Disagreement-Informed Steering for Cultural Alignment), does the same with an unchanged model. The four locals are four personas, the number of blocks is the model's answer to a dilemma, and the locals bargaining over the last bit is an adjustment DISCA makes on top of the average, which it keeps only when the personas mostly agree.

For each country we build four personas from the individual World Values Survey responses: one for each of three age groups (young, middle-aged and older adults) and one for the country as a whole. Each persona is a short paragraph, written in the country's own language, describing values such as religiosity, trust in strangers and views on gender roles at the levels the survey measured for that group. The differences can be surprisingly small. The four US personas differ in just two sentences, about national pride and moral acceptability, and even that narrow spread carries useful information.

We then show the model the same dilemma once under each persona and read off how strongly it prefers each option. The **consensus**, the plain average of the four answers, sets the direction.

But a plain average has a blind spot. Three personas mildly in favour and one strongly against average out to "mildly in favour", so the model ends up somewhere one group firmly rejects. That is why DISCA lets the personas bargain over an adjustment, and how they bargain comes from game theory.

## The whole problem is a game

Here is how that bargaining works. The four personas are the players, and each wants the model's answer to land close to its own view. The model's default answer is what happens if they reach no agreement. Any new answer is a proposed deal, and each player judges it by one question: does this leave me better or worse off than the default?

That is the setup of a **bargaining game**, the problem John Nash formalised in 1950 and Kalai and Smorodinsky later extended: parties with different interests looking for an agreement that each of them prefers to walking away. Game theory offers two classic ways to judge a deal:

- **Utilitarian:** add up how much each player gains. This maximises the total, but it doesn't care who wins and who loses, so a big gain for three players can paper over a big loss for the fourth.
- **Collective:** judge the deal by how well it serves the group as a whole, which here means how well it respects the panel's consensus.

DISCA judges possible answers near the consensus with a blend of the two. To do that, it needs one more ingredient: how a player actually feels about a gain compared with a loss.

### Losing hurts more than winning helps

People don't treat gains and losses symmetrically. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed that a loss feels roughly twice as strong as a gain of the same size: losing 100 dollars hurts more than finding 100 dollars feels good. This is called **loss aversion**, and their **Prospect Theory** captures it with a value curve that rises gently for gains and drops steeply for losses.

DISCA treats each persona as a loss-averse player, and that changes which deals win. Imagine a deal that moves three personas a little closer to their views (+2 each) and pushes the fourth much further away (−3). A plain average calls it a good deal: (2 + 2 + 2 − 3) / 4 = +0.75. With loss aversion, the fourth persona's −3 counts about 2.25 times as much, roughly −6.75, and the same deal scores (2 + 2 + 2 − 6.75) / 4 ≈ −0.2. It gets rejected. (This ignores the curve's slight bend to keep the arithmetic simple.)

So DISCA **adjusts** its answer a little beyond the plain average, toward a compromise that no persona strongly objects to.

## When the panel disagrees, tread lightly

That compromise is only worth trusting when the personas broadly agree. If they are split, say two pulling one way and two the other, any compromise just favours one camp over the other, and with only four voices the result is mostly noise.

So DISCA measures how spread out the four answers are and uses that as a trust dial. When the panel broadly agrees, it keeps the adjustment. The more the panel disagrees, the less of the adjustment it keeps, and when the panel is clearly split it drops the adjustment and stays at the average. This turned out to matter: in our experiments, making the adjustment without checking for disagreement did worse than not adjusting at all, and letting disagreement decide made DISCA less likely to leave any country worse off than before.

The grid is mostly blue, across every region and every model. It isn't blue everywhere, and we show the orange cells on purpose: some country–model pairs get worse, and the smallest model gains the least. One comparison stands out. A 14-billion-parameter model with DISCA ended up closer to people's preferences than a 70-billion-parameter model, five times its size, without it. The same approach also helps beyond car-crash dilemmas, on open-ended moral questions, questions about social norms, and free-form writing, though how much it helps depends on the task.

## Limits

DISCA needs to read how strongly the model prefers each answer option, so it works with models that report these scores, not with ones that only return text. It also needs World Values Survey data for the country in question. And it doesn't win everywhere: the orange cells above are real, and closing them is the next step.

## The takeaway

Aligning a model with "human values" means aligning it with many different people at once. A panel of personas built from public survey data gives a cheap, honest way to hear them. And when they disagree, that disagreement is worth listening to: it tells the model how far it should go.

DISCA is a way to make a model less one-sided, not a claim that the majority is always right.
